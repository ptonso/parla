## brief

The goal is a high-quality, peer-reviewed scientific article. The author supplies the findings and the claims. You supply the craft: the ideas are clear and grounded in evidence, each claim sits next to its support at the strength the evidence allows, and the argument builds in an order a reader can follow. A finished paper lets a peer state its contribution in one sentence after the introduction. The author's plan wins over the usual order of parts.

## parts

| part | job | topics |
|---|---|---|
| abstract | The whole argument as one paragraph: context and its limitation, the approach, the main outcomes, the implication. Written last, from the finished text. | abstract, terminology |
| introduction | Takes the reader from the field to this paper: the problem and why it matters, how the closest work stands and what it leaves open, what this paper contributes. | contributions, flow, claims, citations, terminology |
| method | What was built or done, in the order a reader needs to follow it, with the reason for each design choice. | flow, terminology, figures, captions, citations |
| results | What was measured and under which conditions. Each table and figure is read for the reader, stating what the comparison shows. | tables, figures, captions, claims, flow |
| discussion | What the results mean, where they stop holding, how they bear on the field. | claims, flow, citations |
| conclusion | The claim and what the evidence established, briefly. | claims, flow |
| appendix | Detail a reviewer may want and the argument does not need, each item linked from the text. | flow, figures, tables |
